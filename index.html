The seven modes, compared

The modes are not seven different sets of notes — they are one set of notes started in seven different places. Take the C-major scale and start on D and you get D Dorian; start on E, E Phrygian, and so on. Same notes, different “home.” That is why each mode feels different: the half steps fall in different places against the tonic.

The violin shortcut: to find D Dorian, don’t memorise a new pattern — just play the C-major scale (D Dorian’s parent) but treat D as home. Your fingers already know the notes; only the centre of gravity moves. The “Parent major” line in the explorer tells you that parent for any tonic.

Beyond the modes

Harmonic minor raises the 7th degree (in A minor, G→G♯) to create a strong pull back to the tonic — the sound of classical minor and much film music. Melodic minor raises the 6th and 7th going up, then (in classical practice) lowers them again coming down. Pentatonic scales drop the “risky” notes — major pentatonic omits the 4th and 7th, which is why it almost never clashes. The blues scale adds the flattened 5th (the “blue note”) to minor pentatonic.

Why Locrian is the odd one out

Locrian has a flattened 2nd and a flattened 5th, so its tonic chord is diminished — there’s no stable “home” chord to rest on. Composers use it for fleeting tension, rarely as a whole piece’s home. Try playing it above: it sounds like it keeps wanting to fall somewhere else.

Circle of fifths

Move clockwise and each key is a perfect fifth (7 semitones) above the last, and its signature moves one step toward sharps — adding a sharp, or removing a flat. Move anticlockwise (a fifth below, or a fourth above) and the signature moves one step toward flats — adding a flat, or removing a sharp. Neighbours on the circle share almost all their notes, which is why chord changes between them feel smooth.

Selecting a key on the wheel also sets the tonic everywhere else on this page — the scale explorer, diatonic triads, progressions and the chord & arpeggio explorer all follow it.

Order of flats & sharps: flats are always added in the order B♭ · E♭ · A♭ · D♭ · G♭ · C♭ · F♭ (“Battle Ends And Down Goes Charles' Father”). Sharps run in exactly the reverse order: F♯ · C♯ · G♯ · D♯ · A♯ · E♯ · B♯. So a key signature with 3 flats is always B♭, E♭, A♭ (E♭ major / C minor) — never any other three.

How players actually use it: ① Count sharps/flats to identify a key signature at a glance. ② Find the relative minor instantly (three steps clockwise inside). ③ Plan modulations — moving one step changes only one note, moving across the circle (e.g. C → F♯) changes almost everything and feels dramatic. ④ The I–IV–V chords of any key are three neighbours sitting side by side on the circle. Clockwise is the circle of fifths; read counter-clockwise and the same wheel is the circle of fourths.

Chords & progressions

Stack every other note of a scale and you get its chords. In a major key the pattern of chord qualities is always the same — I major, ii minor, iii minor, IV major, V major, vi minor, vii° diminished — whatever the tonic. Roman numerals name the job a chord does, so the same progression can be played in any key.

Progressions worth knowing by ear

All progressions follow the tonic you chose in the explorer — currently C. Major progressions are in that major key; the two marked “Minor” are in its parallel minor (same tonic, minor key). Press play and follow the highlight — each chord gets about a second and a half.

Reading the numerals: UPPERCASE = major chord, lowercase = minor, ° = diminished. The number is the scale degree the chord is built on. So in C major, ii–V–I is D minor → G major → C major; in G major it’s A minor → D major → G major. Same jobs, different notes — that’s the whole idea.

All scales in D — listen & compare

Every scale and mode from the Scale / mode dropdown above, rebuilt on the same tonic — D — so the starting note never changes and only the pattern changes. That is the fairest way to hear what a mode really does: D Ionian (major) and D Dorian start on the same D, but the half steps fall in different places, so one sounds bright and settled and the other minor but hopeful. Work down the list, or press Play all in order and listen to the whole set back to back.

Anchored in D throughout — D major, the three D minors, D Dorian, D Phrygian and every other variation in the dropdown, each played ascending over one octave (tonic to tonic). Playing a scale here does not change the tonic or scale you have set in the explorer above.

How to listen: keep D fixed in your ear as “home.” In D Ionian the half steps are E–F♯ and C♯–D; move to D Dorian and the F♯ becomes F♮ and the C♯ becomes C♮, while the 6th stays B — that one comparison is the whole idea of modes in miniature. The three minors sit together in the Minor family group: natural (nothing raised), harmonic (7th raised to C♯), and melodic (6th and 7th raised, B and C♯).
